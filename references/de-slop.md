# De-slop pass (long-form)

Blog-length AI writing has its own tells, distinct from short-form social copy. Read the draft back specifically looking for these — don't rely on catching them while drafting.

## Editorializing closers

The single most common tell: a paragraph states a concrete fact, then adds a sentence explaining what the reader should conclude from it.

> "...the pattern is already made when it can be reused across formats. That's the difference between a one-off asset and something that compounds."

Cut the second sentence. The concrete fact — the number, the mechanism, the story beat — should be the last thing on the page. A real person describing their own work states the fact and moves on; explaining its own significance back to the reader is what reads as generated.

This applies to every section, not just the ending of the whole piece — check paragraph endings throughout, not only the final paragraph.

## Tricolon and em-dash pile-ups

"Not just X, but Y" and "X. Y. Z." rhythms, especially stacked close together, are a generation fingerprint. One tricolon in a whole piece can land; three in a row reads as pattern, not voice.

## Generic transitions

"Furthermore," "In conclusion," "It goes without saying," "At the end of the day" — these do no work. If a transition can be deleted without losing meaning, delete it. Real transitions are usually specific: "So the question became..." / "That's when I..." rather than a generic connective.

## Humble-brag disclaimers

Wrapping an accomplishment in a disclaimer before stating it plainly — "I'm not saying this to brag, but..." or "Not to toot my own horn..." State the accomplishment. The disclaimer is the tell, not the accomplishment.

## Corporate jargon

"Leverage," "synergies," "move the needle," "unpack," "double-click on." None of these belong in a personal blog post regardless of how technical the subject is — plain verbs describing what actually happened read as more credible, not less.

## Flowery abstraction standing in for a concrete detail

"This was a transformative journey of growth and discovery" says nothing. If a sentence could apply to almost any project, it's missing the detail that makes this one real — a number, a tool name, an actual decision point, a specific thing someone said.

## Over-explaining a concept the reader likely knows

If the audience already knows what an LLM or a sketch interface is, don't stop to define it. Trust the reader; use the term and move on.

## The test

Read each paragraph and ask: could this have been written by someone who wasn't actually there? If yes, find the specific detail that only the person who lived it would know, and put it back in — usually by re-checking the Step 3 digest, which is where those details live before drafting smooths them out.
