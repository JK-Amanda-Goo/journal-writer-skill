# Choosing a structure

Structure isn't fixed for this skill — the source material already has a shape, and the job in Step 4 is to notice it, not impose a default template. Propose the closest fit, explain why in one line, and let the user override before drafting starts.

## Problem / Process / Results / Learnings (PPRL)

**Shape:** what wasn't working → what was tried → what it produced → what it taught.

**Fits:** project retrospectives, thesis or product presentations, milestone recaps, "here's what I built and why" posts — anything with a clear before-state and a clear after-state, where the reader benefits from seeing the reasoning in between.

**Watch for:** don't force all four sections to be equal length. A post can spend most of its weight on Process and compress Results to a paragraph — the source decides the proportions, the template just orders them.

## Narrative arc

**Shape:** chronological — this happened, then this, then this — building toward a turn or realization near the end.

**Fits:** personal reflections, "how I got here" pieces, journey posts spanning months or years, anything where the *order events happened in* is itself part of the point.

**Watch for:** narrative arc drifts into a bare activity log easily. Keep asking what the through-line is — a narrative without one is just a timeline.

## Launch / update announcement

**Shape:** what shipped → why it matters → what to try → what's next.

**Fits:** product updates, feature releases, "we just launched X" posts. Closer to release notes than to a personal essay — the reader wants to know what changed and what to do about it, not the emotional journey.

**Watch for:** keep it functional. This is the one structure where a bit of "warm but not chatty" (see `amanda-voice`'s content-type notes, if that skill is in use) beats narrative flourish.

## Lessons-learned / discrete takeaways

**Shape:** N specific, standalone lessons, each with its own concrete detail — not a story, a set.

**Fits:** craft posts, skill retrospectives, "things I'd tell myself a year ago" pieces, anything where the source material is naturally a list of separate insights rather than one throughline.

**Watch for:** every item needs its own specific, checkable detail (a real number, a real decision, a real before/after) — a lessons-learned post with vague items ("communication matters", "test early") is skippable. If fewer than 3 items are actually specific, this isn't the right structure — fold what's left into narrative or PPRL instead.

## Question-framed essay

**Shape:** open with a real question → explore it through what was tried/built/lived → land on a reframed version of the question, often turned back on the reader.

**Fits:** reflective pieces where the point isn't a result but a way of thinking about something — closer to an essay than a report. This is the shape of the Doodlely / ITP Thesis Week post this skill's first draft used: "what did you wish to be when you were growing up?" opening, "what originality do you wish to bring?" closing.

**Watch for:** the closing question has to be earned by what came before it, not just aesthetically parallel to the opening — check that the piece actually changed the question, not just repeated it.

## Picking one

Ask, from the gathered material:

1. **Is there a clear before/after or problem/solution shape?** → PPRL.
2. **Does the order things happened in matter, building to a turn?** → Narrative arc.
3. **Is this "we shipped something," functional and reader-actionable?** → Launch/update.
4. **Is the material naturally a set of separate, specific insights rather than one story?** → Lessons-learned.
5. **Is the point a way of seeing something, more than a result?** → Question-framed essay.

More than one can fit — say which two, and why one edges out the other, rather than silently picking. The user knows what kind of piece they set out to write better than the source material alone tells you.
